Twisted Vigenere — GlacierCTF

The challenge supplied an encryption script and a Base64-encoded ciphertext. Despite its name, the encryption was repeating-key XOR: the flag served as the key, repeated across the plaintext.

For each byte, the encryption followed:
\[
C_i=P_i\oplus K_{i\bmod L}
\]
Here, \(C\) is the ciphertext, \(P\) is the plaintext, and \(K\) is the flag of unknown length \(L\).

The unusual part was the plaintext. Instead of ordinary text, the script generated 2,536 bytes using Python’s random.randbytes(). This initially made the ciphertext look difficult to attack because there were no predictable words or sentences.

However, Python’s generator uses MT19937, whose output has mathematical relationships. The attack exploited those relationships rather than guessing the random seed.

After Base64 decoding, the ciphertext contained exactly 2,536 bytes, equivalent to 634 consecutive 32-bit words. MT19937 maintains 624 state words. This meant the challenge exposed a full state’s worth of encrypted output, plus ten additional words that could constrain the hidden key.

MT19937 applies a reversible operation called tempering before returning each output word. Tempering consists of XORs, shifts, and masks, which can be represented as linear operations over individual bits.

For each candidate flag length, every flag bit was treated as an unknown variable. XORing these symbolic key bits with the ciphertext produced symbolic plaintext words. Reversing tempering then expressed the generator’s internal state in terms of those same unknown flag bits.

The solver imposed MT19937’s recurrence between the first state words and the ten additional output words. These relationships supplied 320 bit equations. Another 31 equations enforced consistency within a valid generator state.

The known flag structure supplied further constraints: the key began with gctf{ and ended with }.

All these constraints formed a linear system over \(GF(2)\), where addition is XOR. Gaussian elimination tested candidate key lengths from 6 through 80 bytes, rejecting inconsistent or insufficiently constrained systems.

At 48 bytes, the equations produced a unique solution: the system had rank 384 out of 384 unknown key bits.

The result was then verified beyond checking its appearance. The recovered key decrypted the ciphertext, and the resulting plaintext reconstructed an MT19937 state. A cloned generator reproduced all 2,536 plaintext bytes exactly. Encrypting those bytes again also reproduced the original ciphertext.

The weakness was the combination of a repeating XOR key and plaintext generated by a predictable, algebraically structured generator. The plaintext appeared random, but its internal relationships revealed the flag.

Flag: gctf{from_rand0m_!mpor+_n0+_s0_r4nd0m_af+3r_al1}
